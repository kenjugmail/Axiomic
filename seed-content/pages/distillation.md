# Knowledge Distillation

You have a large, capable model — call it the **teacher**. You want to deploy a smaller, cheaper model that approximates the teacher's behavior — the **student**.

**Distillation** is the family of techniques for transferring capability from teacher to student. Hinton, Vinyals, and Dean (2015) framed the original idea: train the student to match the teacher's *output distribution*, not just the final argmax. The 'soft labels' from the teacher carry richer information than hard labels — they tell you not just what's right but how confident the teacher is and what the alternatives are.

For LLMs, distillation has become how labs build smaller models: train (or fine-tune) the small model to mimic the large model's outputs across a curated dataset. The result: a student that's 10× smaller but retains a meaningful fraction of the teacher's capability.

<!-- tier:undergrad -->
# Knowledge Distillation (Undergrad)

## The basic recipe

For classification (and language modeling, treating it as next-token classification):

1. **Run the teacher** on a corpus to produce soft predictions — full probability distributions over classes / tokens.
2. **Train the student** to minimize the KL divergence between its predictions and the teacher's.
3. Optionally **mix in hard-label loss** (standard cross-entropy on the true label) for robustness.

The standard distillation loss:

$$\mathcal{L} = \alpha \cdot \mathcal{L}_{\text{soft}}(\text{teacher}, \text{student}) + (1 - \alpha) \cdot \mathcal{L}_{\text{hard}}(\text{label}, \text{student})$$

With `α` typically 0.5-0.9. The soft loss carries the teacher's information; the hard loss anchors the student to the actual ground truth.

## Temperature

Hinton's original trick: use a higher **temperature** in the softmax when computing the teacher and student distributions for the soft loss. High temperature flattens the distribution, exposing the teacher's relative confidence on non-top classes — which is the rich signal you want to transfer.

$$P(c) = \frac{\exp(z_c / T)}{\sum_{c'} \exp(z_{c'} / T)}$$

Typical T values: 2-10 during distillation training, then T=1 at inference. Without temperature, the teacher's distribution is often near-one-hot and you lose most of the soft-label information.

## Distillation for LLMs

For language models, distillation is essentially the same idea applied to next-token prediction. Two main flavors:

**Output distillation** (Hinton-style). Train the student on the teacher's full next-token distribution at every position. Loss is per-token KL between student and teacher logits.

**Sequence-level distillation**. Have the teacher generate completions; train the student to reproduce them. This is the dominant flavor for instruction-tuned LLMs — easier to scale, doesn't require both models to have the same vocabulary.

Examples in the wild:

- **DistilBERT** (Sanh et al. 2019). 6-layer student trained from a 12-layer teacher (BERT-base). 40% smaller, 60% faster, retains ~97% of teacher's GLUE performance. Output distillation.
- **Alpaca** (Taori et al. 2023). LLaMA-7B fine-tuned on 52K instruction-following examples generated by GPT-3.5 (a much larger teacher). Sequence-level distillation. Demonstrated that small open models could approximate much larger closed ones at low cost.
- **Phi-3** (Microsoft 2024). 'Textbooks are all you need' approach: train a small model on synthetic high-quality data generated by GPT-4. Effectively distillation, with the teacher's knowledge captured in the curated dataset.

The pattern: distillation lets a small model 'inherit' capability from a larger one cheaply. Whether the small model retains everything is task-dependent.

# Knowledge Distillation (Graduate)

## Why distillation works (when it does)

The standard explanation: the teacher's soft output distribution carries information about the *similarity structure* of the data — which classes look alike, which tokens are reasonable alternatives, where the teacher is uncertain. The student inherits not just the labels but the relationships.

This is consistent with empirical observations:

- Distillation on the same exact dataset that was used to train the teacher usually outperforms training the student from scratch on hard labels.
- Distilled students are often *more calibrated* than from-scratch students — they inherit the teacher's confidence structure.
- Distilled students sometimes generalize better in low-data regimes.

The catch: distillation transfers what the teacher *outputs*, not what the teacher *is*. If the teacher has internal capabilities that aren't expressed in next-token distributions over the distillation corpus, the student doesn't get those.

## Modern variants

**On-policy distillation** (Lin et al. 2020, Agarwal et al. 2023). Instead of training on a fixed corpus, sample from the *student* during training and have the teacher score those samples. Closer in spirit to RL than SFT. Reduces distribution mismatch — the student is trained on the kinds of inputs it will actually generate at inference.

**MiniLLM** (Gu et al. 2024). Reverse KL distillation for LLMs. Standard distillation uses forward KL `D_KL(student || teacher)` which encourages the student to cover all of the teacher's modes. Reverse KL `D_KL(teacher || student)` is mode-seeking — the student matches the teacher's most-likely behavior more aggressively, at the cost of breadth. Useful when you want the student to focus on the teacher's high-quality outputs.

**Distill-Step-by-Step** (Hsieh et al. 2023). Generate not just final answers from the teacher but reasoning chains. Train the student to produce both. Substantially improves student performance on reasoning tasks compared to answer-only distillation.

**Self-distillation**. Train the student on the teacher's outputs, where the teacher is a *previous version* of the same model. Used as a regularization technique; sometimes improves performance.

## When distillation fails

- **Long-tail capabilities**. The teacher's rare-but-correct behaviors (specialized knowledge, niche skills) often don't survive distillation if they're not in the distillation corpus. The student inherits the average behavior, not the tails.
- **Reasoning depth**. Distillation transfers behavior on the distribution it sees. If the teacher's reasoning relies on internal state the student doesn't have capacity for, the student can mimic outputs on training inputs but fail on out-of-distribution reasoning.
- **Misalignment**. Distilling from an aligned teacher to a smaller model usually preserves *some* alignment behavior — but jailbreaks and edge-case behaviors can return. Refusal training transfers imperfectly.
- **Calibration**. Hard-label distillation alone doesn't transfer calibration. Need soft labels (with appropriate temperature) for that.

The honest framing: distillation is a *capability transfer* technique, not a *capability creation* technique. The student can't exceed the teacher in any meaningful sense; it can approximate the teacher cheaply.
