# Entanglement

A purely quantum correlation between qubits. Two qubits are **entangled** if their joint state cannot be written as a product of individual states.

Most 2-qubit states ARE entangled. Product states form a measure-zero subset.

**Bell state Φ⁺ = (|00⟩ + |11⟩)/√2** is the canonical example: measuring either qubit perfectly determines the other.

Entanglement is the resource that powers quantum advantage. Without entanglement, quantum computation is no more powerful than classical (efficient classical simulation via tensor networks).

# Entanglement (Undergrad)

## Defining entanglement

A 2-qubit pure state `|ψ⟩` is **separable** (product) if:
`|ψ⟩ = |ψ_A⟩ ⊗ |ψ_B⟩`

If no such decomposition exists, the state is **entangled**.

Examples:
- Separable: `|00⟩ = |0⟩ ⊗ |0⟩`. Just product of |0⟩ with |0⟩.
- Separable: `(|0⟩+|1⟩)/√2 ⊗ |0⟩`. Each qubit has its own independent state.
- Entangled: `(|00⟩ + |11⟩)/√2`. No product form.

For mixed states, the criterion is more subtle: `ρ` is separable if expressible as a convex combination of product density matrices.

## Why correlations differ from classical

In a classical correlated system: 'either both 0 or both 1, with 50/50 probability'. Measurements reveal correlations.

In an entangled state: same observed correlations BUT the system supports interference + cannot be cloned + violates Bell inequalities.

The differences are subtle but real. Bell-inequality experiments empirically distinguish quantum from classical correlations.

## Bell states

Four maximally entangled 2-qubit states:
- **Φ⁺**: (|00⟩ + |11⟩)/√2.
- **Φ⁻**: (|00⟩ − |11⟩)/√2.
- **Ψ⁺**: (|01⟩ + |10⟩)/√2.
- **Ψ⁻**: (|01⟩ − |10⟩)/√2.

These form an orthonormal basis of the 2-qubit Hilbert space. Used in:
- Quantum teleportation.
- Dense coding.
- Bell-inequality experiments.
- EPR-pair generation for cryptography.

Generated by H + CNOT from |00⟩, |01⟩, |10⟩, |11⟩.

## Multi-qubit entanglement

For 3+ qubits, entanglement becomes complex:
- **GHZ state**: `(|000⟩ + |111⟩)/√3`. Maximally entangled all-or-nothing.
- **W state**: `(|001⟩ + |010⟩ + |100⟩)/√3`. Entangled but more robust.
- These are inequivalent under local operations + classical communication (LOCC).

In `n` qubits, an exponentially-rich variety of entangled states exists. Most are 'highly entangled' in the sense that measuring some qubits leaves the rest in a complex state.

## Entanglement as resource

Entanglement is not generated by single-qubit gates. Need at least one 2-qubit gate (CNOT, CZ, or similar). Once present, single-qubit gates can't 'unentangle' (they can rotate the entangled state but not return to a product state).

For quantum advantage, algorithms must:
1. Generate entanglement.
2. Maintain it during computation (decoherence destroys it).
3. Eventually extract useful information via measurement.

Without entanglement, no advantage — quantum becomes classical.

# Entanglement (Grad)

## Schmidt decomposition

Any pure 2-qubit state has a unique Schmidt decomposition:
`|ψ⟩ = Σ_i √λ_i |u_i⟩|v_i⟩`

where `|u_i⟩, |v_i⟩` are orthonormal sets and `λ_i ≥ 0` are Schmidt coefficients with `Σ λ_i = 1`.

**Schmidt rank**: number of nonzero `λ_i`. Rank 1 = separable; rank 2 = entangled.

**Reduced density matrix**: `ρ_A = Tr_B(|ψ⟩⟨ψ|)`. For entangled `|ψ⟩`, `ρ_A` is mixed.

## Entanglement entropy

Quantifies entanglement:
`S(ρ_A) = -Tr(ρ_A log ρ_A)`

- Product state: `S = 0`.
- Maximally entangled: `S = log(d)` where `d` is the dimension of subsystem.

Entanglement entropy obeys area laws for ground states of local Hamiltonians (in 1D). Important for understanding which quantum systems are classically simulable: low-entanglement states are simulable via tensor networks.

## Bell-inequality violation

CHSH inequality: for any local hidden-variable theory:
`|⟨A_1B_1⟩ + ⟨A_1B_2⟩ + ⟨A_2B_1⟩ - ⟨A_2B_2⟩| ≤ 2`

Quantum mechanics achieves `2√2 ≈ 2.83` (Tsirelson's bound). Experimentally verified to many sigma. Loophole-free experiments (Hensen et al. 2015, Giustina et al. 2015) settled the issue.

Implications:
- The world is NOT locally deterministic.
- Either locality or determinism (or both) abandoned.
- Quantum is fundamentally non-classical.

## Mixed-state entanglement

For mixed states, entanglement is harder to define + detect.

**Separable**: `ρ = Σ p_i ρ_A^i ⊗ ρ_B^i`.

Otherwise entangled. Determining separability is NP-hard in general.

**Entanglement measures** for mixed states:
- **Entanglement of formation**: minimum entanglement to prepare the state from product states + LOCC.
- **Distillable entanglement**: max ratio of pure Bell pairs extractable via LOCC.
- **Negativity**: based on partial transpose; computable.

These don't all coincide for mixed states.

## Monogamy

Entanglement is **monogamous**: if A is maximally entangled with B, A cannot be entangled with any third party C.

This has cryptographic implications: a key shared via entanglement is intrinsically secure — a third party can't have the same correlations.

## Entanglement in algorithms

- **Shor + Grover**: generate substantial entanglement during execution. Entanglement entropy grows with depth.
- **VQE + QAOA**: variational ansätze deliberately produce parameterized entanglement.
- **Quantum simulation**: simulating an entangled physical system uses entanglement to represent it efficiently.
- **Random circuits**: produce highly entangled states; basis of quantum supremacy demonstrations.

The amount of entanglement an algorithm generates determines how classically hard it is.

## Connection to classical simulation

**Matrix product states (MPS)**: efficiently represent low-entanglement states. Classical simulation via DMRG, tensor networks.

For `n` qubits with bond dimension `χ`, MPS has `O(n · χ²)` parameters. If entanglement entropy is bounded (`χ` small), simulation is efficient.

**High-entanglement states**: bond dimension grows exponentially with depth. Classical simulation becomes intractable.

This is why entanglement is the resource: it's what makes classical simulation hard.
