# Quantum Superposition

The defining feature of quantum mechanics: a quantum system can be in a **linear combination** of basis states.

For a qubit:

`|ψ⟩ = α|0⟩ + β|1⟩`

with complex amplitudes `α, β` satisfying `|α|² + |β|² = 1`. The qubit isn't 'really' 0 or 1 — it's both, until measurement collapses it.

Superposition is NOT classical probability. The amplitudes are complex; relative phases produce **interference**. Two superpositions can cancel each other (destructive) or amplify (constructive). Classical probabilities can only add.

(Distinct from `[[superposition]]` in deep-learning interpretability, which describes how features share dimensions in neural-net activations.)

<!-- tier:undergrad -->
# Quantum Superposition (Undergrad)

## Why superposition is more than probability

A classical probabilistic bit could be in state '50% 0, 50% 1'. So is `(|0⟩ + |1⟩)/√2`. What's the difference?

**Apply a unitary**: classically, applying a deterministic operation to a 50/50 random bit gives some other distribution. Quantum: applying H to `(|0⟩ + |1⟩)/√2` gives `|0⟩` with probability 1.

The quantum case has **interference**: amplitudes can cancel. Classical can't.

`H((|0⟩ + |1⟩)/√2) = (H|0⟩ + H|1⟩)/√2`
`= ((|0⟩+|1⟩)/√2 + (|0⟩-|1⟩)/√2)/√2 = |0⟩`

The +|1⟩ and −|1⟩ amplitudes cancel; +|0⟩ and +|0⟩ add. Pure interference.

Classical probabilities have no minus signs. Quantum amplitudes do.

## Multi-qubit superposition

For `n` qubits, superposition can span all `2^n` basis states:

`|ψ⟩ = Σ_{x ∈ {0,1}^n} α_x |x⟩`

A 50-qubit register can be in superposition of `2^50 ≈ 10^15` basis states simultaneously.

Each amplitude is a complex number. Specifying the full state requires `2^n` complex numbers — exponentially more than classical.

**This exponential scaling is the source of quantum advantage**. Algorithms exploit it via interference to compute global properties in time polynomial in `n` rather than exponential.

## Constructive + destructive interference

When amplitudes for the same outcome ADD with the SAME sign (or compatible phases), the probability AMPLIFIES (constructive interference).

When they ADD with OPPOSITE signs (or anti-phases), they CANCEL (destructive interference).

This is the mechanism quantum algorithms exploit:

1. Set up superposition of all possible inputs.
2. Apply unitary that imprints problem structure on amplitudes.
3. Apply transformation causing desired outcomes to interfere CONSTRUCTIVELY and undesired to interfere DESTRUCTIVELY.
4. Measure. Desired outcome observed with high probability.

Grover's algorithm is the cleanest example: each iteration causes a bit more constructive interference at the target state.

## Limits

Superposition isn't infinite resource:

- **Measurement collapses**: each measurement reveals only ONE outcome. Need many shots to extract distribution information.
- **No-cloning**: can't make backup copies of unknown superpositions.
- **Holevo bound**: at most `n` classical bits per `n` qubits transmitted.
- **Decoherence**: real superpositions decay over time as the system interacts with environment.

Superposition gives advantages for COMPUTATION (specific algorithms), not for STORAGE or COMMUNICATION (which are bounded by classical limits).

# Quantum Superposition (Grad)

## Coherence vs decoherence

**Coherence**: maintaining the relative phases of a superposition. Required for interference.

**Decoherence**: loss of coherence due to environmental coupling. The superposition `α|0⟩ + β|1⟩` becomes a CLASSICAL probabilistic mixture: 'with probability `|α|²` it's |0⟩, with `|β|²` it's |1⟩'.

Once decohered, NO interference is possible. The state is classical.

T₂ time characterizes how fast decoherence happens. Long T₂ = longer-lived superposition = deeper achievable algorithms.

## Stabilizer states + classical simulability

A subset of quantum states (stabilizer states) is classically simulable in polynomial time (Gottesman-Knill theorem). These include:
- Computational basis states.
- Bell states.
- Anything reachable from |0⟩^n via Clifford gates (H, S, CNOT, X, Y, Z).

NON-Clifford gates (like T) generate non-stabilizer states — these are harder to simulate. The need for non-Clifford gates is what makes quantum computation classically hard.

This means superposition alone isn't enough for quantum advantage; you need the RIGHT KIND of superposition, generated by gates outside the Clifford group.

## Macroscopic superposition

Superposition is empirically observed for individual qubits. Does it extend to macroscopic systems?

**Schrödinger's cat**: thought experiment about superposition of macroscopic states. Practically, decoherence destroys macroscopic superposition very fast.

**Recent experiments**: superposition demonstrated for ~10^11 atoms, ~10^9 photons, ~10^4-amu molecules. The boundary keeps moving as experiments improve.

Doesn't affect engineering of NISQ-era quantum computers, but is a deep open question in foundations.

## Connection to amplitude amplification

The mechanism Grover's algorithm exploits:

1. Initial uniform superposition has amplitude `1/√N` on each basis state.
2. Target state amplitude is `1/√N` (same as others).
3. Each Grover iteration rotates the state vector toward the target subspace.
4. After ~`(π/4)√N` iterations, the target amplitude is close to 1; non-target close to 0.

This is constructive interference at the target + destructive interference at non-targets, choreographed by the algorithm.
